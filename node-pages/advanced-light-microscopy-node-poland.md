# Advanced Light Microscopy Node Poland

## Overview

The Advanced Light Microscopy Node Poland mainly offers advanced fluorescence microscopy, from confocal and two-photon imaging to super-resolution and functional methods that measure how molecules move and interact. Scanning and volume electron microscopy add ultrastructural detail at one of its sites. The Node has three facilities at three institutions in Krakow and Warsaw, and it works in biological imaging across scales, from molecules and organelles to cells, tissues and whole organisms.

Staff at PL-EuBI-AMF, the imaging core facility of the Nencki Institute of Experimental Biology in Warsaw, are expert in advanced light microscopy and image analysis. They work with cells on slides, dishes and chambered coverslips, and with mouse and rat cranial-window preparations for imaging in living animals. The Jagiellonian University site in Krakow is a cell-biophysics laboratory that combines live-cell fluorescence imaging with cell and molecular biology to study how DNA damage arises and is repaired. Its team has developed its own approach to detecting DNA breaks, together with dedicated software for quantitative analysis. The LLTM microscopy unit in Warsaw, at the Mossakowski Medical Research Institute, brings experience in advanced light microscopy for molecular medicine, cytogenetics and pathology.

Most of the fluorescence microscopy, including confocal, two-photon and STED microscopy, is at PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw. Single-molecule localisation microscopy is at PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw and at the Jagiellonian University site in Krakow. PL-EuBI-AMF and the Jagiellonian University site also both offer fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM), fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) and fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS). Scanning electron microscopy and serial block-face SEM are at PL-EuBI-AMF as well, along with high-content screening.

## Imaging technologies offered

Fluorescence light microscopy makes up most of what the Node offers. At PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw, confocal, spinning disk and deconvolution widefield microscopes give optical sections and 3D localisation of labelled structures in cells and tissues. TIRF microscopy restricts imaging to a thin layer near the coverslip, which gives high contrast for membranes and single molecules. Two-photon microscopy reaches cells deep in thick tissue, and the Zeiss MP system supports imaging in living animals.

For detail below the diffraction limit, PL-EuBI-AMF offers STED microscopy, including on the Abberior MIRAVA POLYSCOPE. Single-molecule localisation microscopy maps the positions of individual molecules. It is offered at the Jagiellonian University site in Krakow and at PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw, where it runs on the ONI Nanoimager S. PL-EuBI-AMF and the Jagiellonian University site also offer FRAP, FCS and FLIM, which show how fast molecules exchange, how they diffuse and how their local environment changes. FRET at PL-EuBI-AMF reports whether two labelled molecules lie within nanometres of each other.

PL-EuBI-AMF also runs high-content screening, which measures many cell phenotypes automatically across large numbers of wells, together with label-free holotomography and laser microdissection. Its scanning electron microscopy, on a Zeiss Sigma, shows surface morphology. Serial block-face SEM at the same facility builds 3D views of the ultrastructure in selected cell and tissue volumes.

## Specialties and research focus

The Node's research spans scales, from molecular and elemental levels through organelle dynamics and cell and tissue processes to whole organisms. At the Jagiellonian University site in Krakow, the work centres on DNA damage and repair, genome stability and chromatin. The team studies cells in DNA-damage and repair experiments, including work on anticancer drugs, and these questions connect to medical diagnostics and the design of therapies.

The LLTM microscopy unit in Warsaw supports research in molecular biology, cytogenetics and molecular medicine. Its work also extends to pathology and forensic medicine, where microscopy helps to examine and select material from tissue samples. PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw images mouse and rat cranial-window preparations, which give the microscope a view into the living brain. Alongside this in vivo work, the facility handles cells and other samples prepared on slides, 35 mm dishes and chambered coverslips. Together the three facilities cover questions from how single molecules behave inside a nucleus to how cells function within an intact tissue.

## Services and user support

A project at the Node usually begins with methodological setup. Staff help design the study protocol and write standard operating procedures, so that the imaging plan fits the biological question from the start. This early planning matters most for methods with many settings to choose, such as lifetime imaging or single-molecule localisation.

During acquisition, staff give technical assistance with running the instruments, and they work through the imaging sessions with the visiting researcher. Wet lab space and a cell culture room are also part of the service, so cells can be cultured and samples prepared on site before they go to the microscope.

Once images are acquired, the Node offers data processing and analysis. This step turns raw image data into measurements that answer the original question, for example molecular mobility values, localisation maps or quantified cell phenotypes. It draws on the image analysis expertise of the staff at PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw and on the quantitative analysis software developed at the Jagiellonian University site in Krakow.

### Services in the signed SLA

- Methodological setup (e.g. design of study protocol and standard operation procedures)
- Technical assistance to run instrument
- Wet lab space and cell culture room
- Data processing and analysis

PL-EuBI-AMF is the imaging core facility of the Nencki Institute of Experimental Biology in Warsaw, and it offers most of the Node's methods. Its staff are expert in advanced light microscopy and image analysis. Their work covers confocal and widefield imaging, two-photon imaging in living animals, super-resolution microscopy, functional fluorescence methods and high-content screening. The same facility also offers scanning and serial block-face electron microscopy, so light and electron imaging are available within one institute.

The Jagiellonian University site in Krakow is a cell-biophysics laboratory. It combines advanced imaging with cell and molecular biology to follow how DNA damage is induced and how cells repair it. Its methods focus on single-molecule localisation microscopy and on fluorescence dynamics, including FRAP, FLIM and FCS. The team has developed its own way of detecting DNA breaks and writes dedicated software to quantify the results.

The LLTM microscopy unit at the Mossakowski Medical Research Institute in Warsaw hosts the Laboratory of Advanced Microscopy Techniques (LAMT). The laboratory belongs to Euro-BioImaging and to the Bio-Imaging Poland consortium. Its staff are experienced in advanced light microscopy and apply it in molecular biology, cytogenetics, molecular medicine, pathology and forensic medicine.
