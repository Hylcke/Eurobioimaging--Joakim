# Advanced Light Microscopy Node Poland

## Overview

The Advanced Light Microscopy Node Poland is built around fluorescence microscopy that goes beyond standard imaging, with particular strength in super-resolution and in methods that measure how molecules move and interact in cells. Most of these technologies are offered by PL-EuBI-AMF, the imaging core facility of the Nencki Institute of Experimental Biology in Warsaw, which also provides scanning electron microscopy. The Node focuses on biological imaging and has three facilities at three institutions in two cities. Alongside the Nencki core, it includes the Jagiellonian University site in Krakow and the LLTM microscopy unit at the Mossakowski Medical Research Institute in Warsaw. Together they support research from the molecular level through organelles and cells to tissues and whole organisms.

Staff at PL-EuBI-AMF specialise in advanced light microscopy and image analysis. They work with cells on slides, dishes and chambered coverslips, and with mouse and rat cranial-window preparations. The Jagiellonian University site is a cell-biophysics laboratory that combines imaging with cell and molecular biology to study how DNA damage arises and is repaired. Its team has developed its own approaches for detecting DNA breaks in cells and has dedicated software for quantitative analysis. The LLTM microscopy unit brings experience in advanced light microscopy applied to molecular medicine and pathology.

Cell biologists who need to resolve structures below the diffraction limit, or to measure molecular mobility and interactions, will find the broadest support at PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw. The same facility suits groups working with rodent cranial-window preparations and those planning image-based screens. Researchers studying genome stability, chromatin or the effects of anticancer drugs should look to the Jagiellonian University site in Krakow. Projects in cytogenetics, pathology or forensic medicine connect most closely with the LLTM microscopy unit.

## Imaging technologies offered

Super-resolution fluorescence is the Node's strongest area. PL-EuBI-AMF offers stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy, which confines the fluorescent spot so that subcellular structures can be resolved directly. Single-molecule localisation microscopy (SMLM) is offered by both PL-EuBI-AMF and the Jagiellonian University site, and it maps individual labelled molecules to show their distribution below ordinary optical resolution.

The same two facilities offer functional methods that measure what molecules do rather than only where they are. Fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM) adds contrast that is sensitive to the molecular environment and to interactions. Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) measures mobility and exchange in a chosen region, while fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS/FCCS) gives diffusion, concentration and co-diffusion of labelled molecules. PL-EuBI-AMF adds FRET, which reports whether two molecules lie within nanometres of each other.

For everyday and three-dimensional imaging, PL-EuBI-AMF runs laser scanning confocal, spinning disk confocal and deconvolution widefield microscopes. Its TIRF microscopy gives high contrast at membranes near the coverslip, and two-photon microscopy reaches cells deep within thick tissue. Automated high-content screening measures phenotypes across many wells and conditions. The facility also offers label-free phase imaging, laser microdissection, scanning electron microscopy and serial block-face SEM for ultrastructure in 3D.

The Abberior MIRAVA POLYSCOPE combines confocal, STED and lifetime imaging in one system. The ONI Nanoimager S handles single-molecule localisation and FRET, and electron imaging runs on a Zeiss Sigma SEM.

## Specialties and research focus

The Node covers life-science questions across scales, from molecular and elemental levels to the dynamics of organelles, the behaviour of cells and tissues, and whole organisms. Each facility brings its own research communities to this range.

At the Jagiellonian University site, research centres on DNA damage and repair. The laboratory studies how breaks arise in the genome and how cells deal with them, with work on genome stability, chromatin organisation and the action of anticancer drugs. Its samples are cells used in DNA-damage and repair studies, and the findings feed into questions relevant to medical diagnostics and to the design of therapies.

The LLTM microscopy unit serves molecular biology and molecular medicine, together with cytogenetics, pathology and forensic medicine. This links the Node to clinically oriented and diagnostic research.

PL-EuBI-AMF works with both cultured cells and animal preparations. Cell samples arrive on slides, 35 mm dishes and chambered coverslips, and the facility also images mouse and rat cranial-window preparations, which bring living rodent tissue within reach of the microscope.

## Services and user support

A project with the Node usually begins with methodological setup. Staff help design the study protocol and write standard operating procedures, so the imaging approach fits the biological question before any data are collected. This planning matters most for quantitative methods such as lifetime imaging or correlation spectroscopy, where the experimental design decides what can be measured.

Once the plan is in place, staff provide technical assistance to run the instruments. A visiting researcher can therefore work with advanced systems such as super-resolution or functional microscopes with an experienced operator alongside.

The Node also offers wet lab space and a cell culture room. Visiting groups can prepare and maintain their samples on site, which helps with live-cell experiments where cells need to be imaged soon after preparation.

After acquisition, staff support data processing and analysis. This covers turning raw images and measurements into quantitative results, which is often the step that takes longest in super-resolution, screening and fluorescence spectroscopy projects.

### Services offered

- Methodological setup (e.g. design of study protocol and standard operation procedures)
- Technical assistance to run instrument
- Wet lab space and cell culture room
- Data processing and analysis

PL-EuBI-AMF in Warsaw is the Node's core facility and the only site that covers both light and electron microscopy. It holds nearly all of the Node's fluorescence methods, from confocal and widefield imaging to super-resolution, screening and functional assays. Because scanning electron microscopy sits in the same facility, a project can move from fluorescence imaging to surface or volume ultrastructure without changing site. It also offers laser microdissection, which links regions selected under the microscope to later molecular analysis.

The Jagiellonian University site in Krakow is a smaller research laboratory in cell biophysics rather than a general core. Its offer is focused on single-molecule localisation and on lifetime, recovery and correlation measurements, which it applies to DNA damage and chromatin. These methods overlap with those at PL-EuBI-AMF, so a project can draw on the biological expertise in Krakow and the wider instrument base in Warsaw.

The LLTM microscopy unit is part of the Mossakowski Medical Research Institute, Polish Academy of Sciences, in Warsaw. It hosts the Laboratory of Advanced Microscopy Techniques, which belongs to both Euro-BioImaging and the Bio-Imaging Poland consortium. The unit contributes expertise in advanced light microscopy for medical and diagnostic research, and it shares a city with the Nencki core. Two of the Node's three facilities are therefore in Warsaw, with the third in Krakow.
