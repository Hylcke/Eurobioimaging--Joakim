# Austrian BioImaging Node / CMI

## Overview

Austrian BioImaging/CMI (Correlated Multimodality Imaging) links light microscopy, electron microscopy and preclinical imaging across Austria. Its main strength is in combining these methods, so that one sample can be characterised across scales, from whole organisms down to cells and molecular structures. The Node has 19 facilities at eight institutions in four cities. Most are in Vienna, and there are further sites in Klosterneuburg and Himberg and at the TIMed CENTER of the University of Applied Sciences Upper Austria. The Node covers both biological and biomedical imaging. Light microscopy is offered at nine facilities, while most preclinical modalities are concentrated at the Preclinical Imaging Laboratories (PIL) in Vienna.

Projects here start from the research question rather than from a fixed technique, and staff help build imaging pipelines that link several modalities. The Imaging & Optics Facility (IOF) in Klosterneuburg works on automation and image analysis. Its staff have adapted a confocal microscope with a vertical stage to follow growing plant roots over long periods. Staff at the VBCF Electron Microscopy Facility in Vienna handle material ranging from viruses and bacteria to whole tissues, using chemical fixation as well as high-pressure and plunge freezing. PIL has its expertise in multimodal preclinical imaging. VetImaging in Vienna combines X-ray microscopy with image processing and visualisation. The Biomedical Image Informatics Group at VRVis in Vienna develops computational methods, including AI, for analysing medical images.

Cell biologists who need super-resolution or long time-lapse imaging will find the widest choice of fluorescence methods at IOF. Structural and cell biologists studying ultrastructure, from immunogold labelling to cryo-EM, should start at the VBCF Electron Microscopy Facility. Translational studies in oncology, cardiovascular research or neuroradiology suit PIL, which integrates several modalities in one workflow. Plant scientists can combine automated phenotyping with long-term root imaging at IOF.

## Imaging technologies offered

Light microscopy is the Node's broadest family. Confocal microscopy for multichannel 3D imaging of cells and tissues is offered by IOF in Klosterneuburg and by CIUS, VetImaging and MedUni Vienna Core Facility Imaging in Vienna. IOF has the widest set of advanced methods, from two-photon imaging in thick tissue to STED and single-molecule localisation microscopy (SMLM), which resolve detail below the diffraction limit. MedUni Vienna Core Facility Imaging also offers SMLM, along with spinning disk and TIRF microscopy for fast or surface-selective imaging. Its instruments include a Zeiss LSM 980 Airyscan 2 and an ONI Nanoimager. FRET, which reports molecular proximity, is offered by MedUni Vienna Core Facility Imaging and VBCF Advanced Microscopy.

PIL offers micro-PET, micro-SPECT and micro-MRI at low and high field, so tracer biology can be read alongside anatomy and physiology. Its Bruker BioSpec 9.4 T/30 supports combined PET/MRI, and PET/CT runs on a Mediso nanoScan 122S. Micro-ultrasound and in vivo optical imaging at PIL add measurements of vessels, blood flow and reporter signals in intact animals. VetImaging offers micro-CT of animals and of ex vivo specimens.

Scanning electron microscopy is offered at four facilities. The VBCF Electron Microscopy Facility adds TEM of chemically fixed samples and Tokuyasu immunogold labelling, while CIUS offers immunogold labelling on resin sections. Cryo-TEM and cryo-FIB thinning are available at the VBCF Electron Microscopy Facility in Vienna and the Electron Microscopy Facility in Klosterneuburg.

## Specialties and research focus

The research these facilities serve runs from molecules to whole organisms. PIL supports translational in vivo, ex vivo and in vitro studies in oncology, cardiovascular research, neuroradiology, pharmacokinetics and musculoskeletal research. The Center for Biomedical Research and Translational Surgery serves cardiovascular research, translational surgery and device testing. Its hybrid operating room in Himberg supports large-animal projects, and a second facility is on the AKH campus in Vienna. MMCT in Vienna works on cancer therapeutics, gene and nucleic-acid delivery and the preclinical evaluation of treatments.

At the cellular scale, VBCF Advanced Microscopy serves cell and developmental biology. The VBCF Electron Microscopy Facility studies the ultrastructure of molecules, viruses, bacteria, organelles, cells and tissues, and the Electron Microscopy Facility in Klosterneuburg is oriented towards structural biology. The PHENOPlant Facility in Vienna measures growth and stress traits in Arabidopsis, crop plants and trees, including responses to drought and climate stress. AIC in Vienna serves bio and polymer analytics, metabolomics, bioprocess analysis and the study of nanoparticles and viruses. The Clinical Unit of Diagnostic Imaging at Vetmeduni in Vienna works in veterinary clinical diagnostics.

## Services and user support

A project usually begins with methodological set-up, where staff help design the study protocol and the standard operating procedures. Researchers can then be trained to use the infrastructure, with training workstations and a training seminar room available. Others prefer to work with technical assistance from staff, who run the instruments alongside them.

For in vivo studies the Node provides animal facilities and animal preparation. Probe preparation and imaging probes support labelling experiments. A tracer development and radiopharmacy facility with a cyclotron supplies the tracers needed for nuclear imaging. Wet lab space and cell culture facilities let visiting researchers prepare their samples on site, and the Node also offers housing facilities. Once images are acquired, staff help with data processing and analysis, which turns raw images into quantitative results. Data storage and server space are provided for the resulting datasets.

The Node combines a few large platforms with specialist units around them. In Vienna, MedUni Vienna Core Facility Imaging covers 2D, 3D and 4D light microscopy of fixed and living cells, from whole tissue to subcellular detail. CIUS at the University of Vienna combines confocal microscopy with SEM, cryo-SEM and immunogold methods, so a project that needs both light and electron microscopy can stay in one facility. VetImaging at Vetmeduni pairs confocal microscopy with micro-CT, which suits work linking tissue anatomy to cellular detail.

In Klosterneuburg, IOF and the Electron Microscopy Facility both belong to ISTA, so fluorescence imaging and cryo-EM can be planned together at one institute. VBCF runs three facilities for advanced light microscopy, electron microscopy and plant phenotyping, alongside its Histology Facility, whose staff specialise in tissue preparation for model-organism research. Smaller units add specialist methods. The Austrian BioImaging MedUni Vienna technology units offer coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) microscopy and serial block-face fluorescence imaging of large tissue volumes. AIC at TU Wien offers Raman spectroscopy, mass spectrometry imaging and atomic force microscopy for chemical and surface analysis. The TIMed CENTER Core Facilities, at sites in Linz, Hagenberg, Wels and Steyr, offer SMLM and atomic force microscopy. USTEM at TU Wien is a service centre for transmission and analytical electron microscopy. The Biomedical Image Informatics Group at VRVis provides medical image analysis.
