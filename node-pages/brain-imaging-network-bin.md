# Brain Imaging Network (BIN)

## Overview

The Brain Imaging Network (BIN) is Portugal's Node for positron emission tomography (PET) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, with a particular focus on the brain. Its main strength is combining the two methods, so that one subject can have PET and MR scans on the same day and in the same visit. The Node consists of a single facility, ICNAS in Coimbra, which belongs to the University of Coimbra. It covers biomedical imaging in both humans and animals, with clinical scanners, a preclinical imaging facility and support for population imaging studies.

Staff at ICNAS are experienced in multimodal MR and PET imaging, in radiochemistry and in machine-learning approaches to imaging data. A local cyclotron and radiopharmacy produce PET tracers matched to specific research questions, with a focus on fluorine-18, carbon-11, gallium-68, copper-64 and nitrogen-13. Because carbon-11 tracers are short-lived, two PET scans can sometimes fit into one visit alongside one or more MR scans. The team also develops new radiolabelled tracer molecules for processes that are not yet studied with PET. On the MR side, a research agreement with the scanner's industrial provider lets staff run work-in-progress sequences, including multinuclear approaches.

Clinical neuroscientists studying Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease or multiple sclerosis will find the closest fit, because these conditions sit at the centre of the Node's work. Projects that need a new or tailored PET tracer make the most of ICNAS, since tracer production and imaging happen in the same building. Groups in oncology and cardiology can also follow a question from animal models on the preclinical scanners to human subjects on the clinical PET and MR systems. Cohort studies can draw on the Node's support for population imaging.

## Imaging technologies offered

Human PET and MR imaging form the core of the Node. MRI and MR spectroscopy run on a Siemens Trio Tim 3T MRI, which shows soft-tissue anatomy, tissue physiology and metabolite levels in chosen regions of the brain or body. PET measures where a radiotracer goes and how fast it moves, which makes it a sensitive readout of specific molecular processes. PET can be combined with MRI to place the tracer signal in its anatomical and physiological context, and PET with CT is also offered.

The preclinical facility repeats this pairing for animal models. A Bruker BioSpec 9.4T animal MRI provides high-field MR imaging and spectroscopy of living animals and of excised specimens. A prototype RPC-PET small-animal system, with a resolution of 0.4 mm, maps tracer distribution in animal organs, and micro-PET/MRI joins the two readouts in one study. Supporting methods include micro-ultrasound for quick structural and blood-flow observations and photoacoustic imaging for vascular contrast. In vivo optical imaging adds fluorescent or bioluminescent reporter measurements in intact animals. For larger studies, advanced support for population imaging covers cohort design, secure data preparation and storage, processing and tailored software.

## Specialties and research focus

Research at BIN centres on the brain. Clinical neuroscience studies at ICNAS address Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease and multiple sclerosis. In these conditions a PET tracer can report on a particular molecular process, while MR adds structure and physiology from the same subject on the same day.

Oncology and cardiology are the other main fields the Node serves. In both, the combination of tracer imaging and MR suits questions about how tissue function and metabolism relate to anatomy. Machine-learning approaches are applied across these three areas to extract more from multimodal datasets.

Radiotracer development is a research field in its own right at ICNAS. The aim is to create radiolabelled molecules for biological processes that PET cannot yet measure. Work with short-lived tracers such as carbon-11 also shapes study design, because it allows repeated PET measurements within a single visit. The facility works with human subjects and with animal models, so a research question can be studied in the clinic and in the laboratory within one institute.

## Services and user support

A project at BIN usually begins with methodological setup, where staff help design the study protocol and the standard operating procedures that go with it. For studies in human subjects, the Node also manages regulatory affairs and arranges clinical trial insurance contracts, which takes much of the administrative work off the research team.

During acquisition, staff provide technical assistance to run the instruments. Researchers who want to operate equipment themselves can be trained in using the infrastructure, with training workstations and a seminar room set aside for this. Preclinical projects can use the Node's animal facilities and housing facilities, so animals can be kept on site between imaging sessions.

Once data are collected, staff help with data processing and analysis. Server space is available for storing the resulting datasets.

ICNAS, the Institute for Nuclear Sciences Applied to Health in Coimbra, is the Node's only facility. It is a medical imaging research institution of the University of Coimbra that brings the main medical imaging modalities together in one building. Alongside the clinical scanners, the same building houses a cyclotron, radiochemistry laboratories, a GMP production facility for PET tracers and a preclinical imaging facility.

This layout is what makes the Node's approach possible. Short-lived tracers can travel from the cyclotron to the scanner within their useful lifetime, and a subject can move between PET and MR rooms in one visit. The preclinical and clinical sides share expertise in tracer chemistry and MR methods, so a study designed in animal models can be carried forward to human imaging without changing institution. A local clinical trials unit supports researchers in their dealings with regulators.

The facility suits projects in molecular, multimodal and translational imaging, particularly brain imaging. It also suits work in oncology, cardiology and radiotracer development, where its staff combine radiopharmacy, MR physics and data analysis under one roof.
