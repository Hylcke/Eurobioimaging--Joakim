# NorMIC Oslo - Advanced Light Microscopy Node

## Overview

NorMIC Oslo is an advanced light microscopy Node built around live imaging of cells in culture. Both of its facilities offer laser scanning confocal, spinning disk confocal and total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy, and most light-microscopy units have incubators that keep temperature and CO2 stable. The Node has two facilities at two institutions in Oslo. One is at the Department of Biosciences, University of Oslo, at Blindern, and the other is at the Institute for Cancer Research, Oslo University Hospital, at Montebello. Its work lies in biological imaging, and alongside light microscopy the Node gives access to immuno-electron microscopy and correlative light and electron microscopy.

Staff provide expertise and technical support across confocal, spinning disk and TIRF microscopy. The team at the NorMIC imaging facility in Oslo (Blindern) is experienced in subcellular imaging of living and fixed cells. They follow vesicle traffic, membrane fusion and fission, cell division, cell migration and immune-cell interactions, and they also image plants, zebrafish and small organs. At Advanced Light Microscopy in Oslo (Montebello), the staff's scope extends to super-resolution and photokinetic experiments, which measure how fluorescent molecules move and exchange inside cells.

Cell biologists studying membrane traffic, mitosis or cell motility in living cells will find most support at the NorMIC imaging facility. Immunologists who want to watch immune cells meet and interact will also be well placed there. Projects that measure molecular mobility or proximity, or that need molecules mapped below the diffraction limit, suit Advanced Light Microscopy, which offers FRAP, FRET and single-molecule localisation microscopy within a cancer research institute.

## Imaging technologies offered

Confocal fluorescence microscopy is the core of the Node, and both facilities offer laser scanning and spinning disk systems. Laser scanning confocal microscopy gives optical sections for multichannel 3D localisation in cells and thin tissues. Spinning disk confocal microscopy sections faster and with less light on the sample, which makes it the usual choice for long time-lapse work on living cells. Both facilities also offer TIRF microscopy, which lights only a thin layer next to the coverslip, so events at the plasma membrane stand out with high contrast. Image scanning microscopy, available at both sites, uses spatially resolved detection to recover finer detail from the same light.

Advanced Light Microscopy adds several methods of its own. Deconvolution widefield microscopy sharpens widefield images computationally, and single-molecule localisation microscopy maps the positions of individual labelled molecules below ordinary resolution limits. FRAP measures how quickly molecules exchange after a local bleach, while FRET reports when two labelled molecules sit within nanometres of each other.

Instruments at the NorMIC imaging facility include an Olympus SpinSR SoRa, an Andor DragonFly and a Leica TIRF microscope. Advanced Light Microscopy runs an LSM 780 confocal. Through the Node, researchers can also reach transmission electron microscopy of chemically fixed samples, immuno-gold labelling on resin or thawed cryo-sections, and pre-embedding correlative light and electron microscopy.

## Specialties and research focus

The research served by the Node centres on how cells organise and move their contents. At the NorMIC imaging facility, the main questions concern the kinetics of intracellular vesicle traffic and the membrane fusion and fission events that drive it. The same facility supports studies of cell division and cell migration, where processes unfold over minutes to hours and must be followed in living cells.

Immune-cell interactions form a second strand of work at the NorMIC imaging facility, alongside imaging of fixed samples for structural and localisation studies. Most of this work uses tissue culture, but the facility also images plants, zebrafish and small organs, so projects are not limited to cultured cell lines.

Advanced Light Microscopy sits within the Institute for Cancer Research, and its work takes place in the context of cancer research. Its photokinetic and super-resolution methods fit questions about how molecules behave and cluster in cancer cells. Where a question needs the molecular detail of electron microscopy, the Node's immuno-gold methods place labelled targets within the ultrastructure of cells and tissues.

## Services and user support

A project usually begins with methodological setup, where staff help design the study protocol and the standard operating procedures for the experiment. Researchers then receive training in using the instruments, supported by training workstations and a training seminar room. During imaging, staff can give technical assistance to run the instruments, so visitors who are less familiar with a method still get reliable data.

The Node also helps with the work that comes before imaging. Staff can assist with probe preparation, and visiting researchers can use wet lab space and cell culture facilities at safety level 1 to prepare their samples close to the microscopes. Housing facilities are part of the support as well. For projects with regulatory requirements, the Node offers a regulatory affairs management service.

Once images are acquired, staff support data processing and analysis, which helps turn time-lapse and super-resolution datasets into measurements. Server space is provided for storing data during the project.

NorMIC Oslo combines two light microscopy core facilities at different institutions, which share a common set of confocal, spinning disk, TIRF and image scanning methods. This overlap means that a project can often start at one site and continue at the other with comparable imaging.

The NorMIC imaging facility is the core facility of the Department of Biosciences, University of Oslo, at Blindern. It has a strong focus on live imaging and runs several spinning disk systems alongside laser scanning confocal and TIRF microscopes. This makes it a good fit for projects that follow dynamic processes in living cells or small organisms, and for groups that also need careful imaging of fixed samples. Its analysis workstations support work on the data after acquisition.

Advanced Light Microscopy is the core facility of the Institute for Cancer Research at the Norwegian Radium Hospital, part of Oslo University Hospital at Montebello. It covers the shared confocal and TIRF methods and adds deconvolution widefield microscopy, single-molecule localisation microscopy and the photokinetic assays FRAP and FRET. A researcher who has characterised a process by live imaging at Blindern can therefore move to Montebello to measure molecular mobility or proximity in the same system. The facility also offers slide scanning and other widefield instruments listed in the table below.
