# NorMIC Oslo - Advanced Light Microscopy Node

## Overview

NorMIC Oslo is the Norwegian Advanced Light Microscopy Node, and it mainly offers fluorescence light microscopy of living and fixed cells. Its focus is live imaging of tissue culture, and most of its light microscopes have incubators that keep temperature and CO2 stable during long experiments. The Node has two facilities in Oslo, one at the Department of Biosciences of the University of Oslo at Blindern and one at the Institute for Cancer Research of Oslo University Hospital in Montebello, and it works in biological imaging.

The staff give expertise and technical support in confocal laser scanning, spinning disk confocal and total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy. The team at NorMIC imaging facility specialises in subcellular imaging of living and fixed cells. They follow vesicle traffic, membrane fusion and fission, cell division and cell migration, and they image how immune cells interact with each other. Their work also extends to plants, zebrafish and small organs. At Advanced Light Microscopy, staff work with live-cell imaging, super-resolution and photokinetic methods, which measure how fluorescent molecules move and exchange inside a cell. The Node also gives access to immuno-electron microscopy techniques.

Confocal, spinning disk and TIRF microscopy are offered by both Advanced Light Microscopy in Montebello and NorMIC imaging facility at Blindern. Image scanning microscopy, which sharpens confocal images by making better use of the detected light, is also at both facilities. Single-molecule localisation microscopy and deconvolution widefield microscopy are at Advanced Light Microscopy, which also offers the functional methods FRAP and FRET. Transmission electron microscopy, immunogold labelling and pre-embedding correlative light and electron microscopy complete the Node's offer.

## Imaging technologies offered

Confocal microscopy is the core of the Node. Laser scanning confocal microscopes cut thin optical sections through cells and tissues, so researchers can locate several labels in three dimensions. Spinning disk confocal microscopes collect sections faster and with less light, which helps when living cells must be followed for hours. TIRF microscopy lights only a thin layer next to the coverslip, so it shows events at the cell membrane with high contrast. Both facilities offer these methods, together with image scanning microscopy for finer detail with standard labels. Instruments include a Zeiss LSM880, an Olympus SpinSR SoRa and an Andor DragonFly at NorMIC imaging facility, and an LSM 780 with a CSU-W1 spinning disk at Advanced Light Microscopy.

Advanced Light Microscopy adds methods for finer structure and for molecular behaviour. Single-molecule localisation microscopy maps the positions of individual labelled molecules below the ordinary resolution limit. FRAP bleaches a small region and measures how quickly fluorescent molecules return, which shows their mobility and exchange. FRET reports when two labelled molecules come within nanometres of each other. Deconvolution widefield microscopy removes blur computationally from widefield images of cells and thin tissue.

The Node also offers electron microscopy of chemically fixed samples for cellular ultrastructure. Immunogold labelling on resin sections or Tokuyasu cryo-sections places molecules within that ultrastructure, and pre-embedding correlative light and electron microscopy links a fluorescent signal to the same region in the electron microscope.

## Specialties and research focus

The research at the Node centres on how cells move material, divide and interact. At NorMIC imaging facility, projects study the kinetics of intracellular vesicle traffic and the fusion and fission of membranes. Other projects follow cells through division and track how they migrate. The facility also studies interactions between immune cells, which often means imaging contacts between living cells over time.

NorMIC imaging facility works with live cells and tissues as well as fixed samples, so a project can combine dynamic recordings with detailed imaging of stained material. Beyond cultured cells, its team images plants, zebrafish and small organs, which brings tissue-level questions into the same subcellular approach.

Advanced Light Microscopy sits within the Institute for Cancer Research at Oslo University Hospital, and its work supports cancer research. Its focus on live-cell imaging and photokinetics links to questions about how molecules behave inside cancer cells. Across the Node, the shared emphasis on tissue culture means that cultured mammalian cells kept at stable temperature and CO2 are the most common samples.

## Services and user support

A project at the Node usually begins with methodological setup, where staff help design the study protocol and the standard operating procedures for the experiment. Researchers then receive training in using the infrastructure, supported by training workstations and a training seminar room. During imaging, staff give technical assistance with running the instruments.

Sample work is supported in several ways. Staff help with probe preparation, and visiting researchers can use wet lab space and cell culture facilities at safety level 1. Housing facilities are also part of the offer. For projects that involve regulatory requirements, the Node provides a regulatory affairs management service.

Once images are acquired, staff help with data processing and analysis, so that recordings of live cells and fixed samples can be turned into measurements. Server space is available for storing data during the project, which keeps large time-lapse and three-dimensional datasets close to the analysis tools.

NorMIC imaging facility at the Department of Biosciences, University of Oslo, is a core facility for advanced light microscopy with a strong focus on live imaging. It offers laser scanning and spinning disk confocal microscopy, TIRF microscopy and image scanning microscopy. Its staff are experienced in subcellular imaging of living and fixed cells, and they follow intracellular dynamics such as vesicle traffic, membrane fusion and fission, cell division and migration. The team also images immune-cell interactions and works with plants, zebrafish and small organs alongside cultured cells. Analysis workstations at the facility support the processing of the images it produces.

Advanced Light Microscopy is a core facility at the Institute for Cancer Research of Oslo University Hospital, at the Norwegian Radium Hospital in Montebello. It provides light-microscopy services across confocal, spinning disk and TIRF microscopy, and it also offers image scanning microscopy. Its staff work with live-cell imaging and super-resolution, including single-molecule localisation microscopy. The facility is also where the Node offers deconvolution widefield microscopy and the photokinetic methods FRAP and FRET, which measure how molecules move and how closely they interact. Its work supports cancer research within the hospital's research institute.

The two facilities share a core set of confocal and TIRF methods. Advanced Light Microscopy adds super-resolution and molecular measurements, while NorMIC imaging facility brings experience with dynamic processes in cells, tissues and small organisms.
