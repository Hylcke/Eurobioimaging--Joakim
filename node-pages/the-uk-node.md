# The UK Node

## Overview

The UK Node is strongest in advanced fluorescence light microscopy, with particular depth in super-resolution and live-cell imaging. Twenty of its facilities offer light microscopy, and several sites add volume electron microscopy, correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) and image analysis. The Node brings together 32 facilities at 15 institutions across 11 locations, from Edinburgh and Newcastle upon Tyne to Oxford and London. It covers biological imaging of molecules, cells and tissues, with biomedical work in disease models, human tissue and living animals.

Super-resolution expertise is concentrated at ESRIC in Edinburgh, whose staff help researchers design experiments as well as run them. Its Heriot-Watt University site offers STED and single-molecule localisation, and its University of Edinburgh site focuses on single-molecule localisation and fast live-cell super-resolution. Staff at the Microscopy Innovation Centre in London develop microscopy instrumentation and analysis tools. The COMPARE Advanced Imaging Facility in Birmingham runs custom as well as commercial microscopes. The team at the University of Liverpool Centre for Cell Imaging in Liverpool is experienced in long-term live-cell acquisition, and HyBiFa in Leeds specialises in label-free imaging based on coherent Raman scattering.

Cell biologists who need nanoscale detail in fixed or living cells will find the most specialised support at ESRIC in Edinburgh. Projects that follow a fluorescent signal into its ultrastructure, or that need cells and tissues in three dimensions at electron microscopy resolution, suit the Babraham Institute Imaging Facility on the Babraham Research Campus near Cambridge. Researchers in immunology, inflammation, cancer and cardiovascular biology who want to watch cells inside living animals should look to the University of Birmingham Intravital Microscopy Suite.

## Imaging technologies offered

Laser scanning confocal microscopy is the Node's most widely offered method, available at twelve facilities. Image scanning microscopy and spinning disk confocal microscopy are each offered at six facilities. Together they give optical sectioning and multichannel 3D localisation in cells and tissues, and the spinning disk systems allow faster, gentler imaging of live samples. Fluorescence lifetime imaging (FLIM) at eight facilities adds contrast that reports on a molecule's environment and interactions. FRAP, FRET and fluorescence correlation spectroscopy measure mobility, proximity and diffusion at several further sites.

For nanoscale detail, single-molecule localisation microscopy maps molecular distributions at eight facilities. STED is offered by ESRIC (EBI-FAC-0332), the Micron BioImaging Facility and the Newcastle University Bioimaging Unit. Light-sheet mesoscopic imaging at six facilities and two-photon microscopy at five reach into embryos, organs and thick tissue. The Oxford-ZEISS Centre of Excellence in Biomedical Imaging adds lattice light-sheet for 3D dynamics in single cells. Intravital microscopy and photomanipulation are offered by the University of Birmingham Intravital Microscopy Suite.

On the electron side, serial block-face SEM is offered at three facilities and FIB-SEM at the Babraham Institute Imaging Facility and the OCTOPUS imaging cluster. The Babraham Institute Imaging Facility also offers array tomography and live-cell CLEM. Cryo-EM methods, label-free harmonic and Raman imaging, atomic force microscopy and spatial omics complete the picture. Instruments include a Leica SP8 gSTED/TauSTED/FLIM and a Nikon N-STORM at the Edinburgh sites. Oxford has a Zeiss Lattice Lightsheet 7, and the Birmingham intravital suite runs an Olympus FVMPE-RS multiphoton.

## Specialties and research focus

The Node's facilities serve research that runs from single molecules to whole organisms. At the Astbury Biostructure Laboratory Electron Microscopy Facility in Leeds, structural biology covers membrane proteins, ribosomes, viruses and tissues, with examples in Alzheimer's disease and DNA repair. CICF in Oxford supports studies of iPSC-derived neuronal organoids, mitochondrial tracking, wound healing, cell migration and macrophage motility. In Birmingham, the Intravital Microscopy Suite works on thrombosis, inflammation, cancer and the recruitment of immune cells in liver, heart and kidney. The Oxford-ZEISS Centre of Excellence in Biomedical Imaging serves inflammatory and musculoskeletal research, and the COMPARE Advanced Imaging Facility addresses questions such as receptor signalling.

Some facilities work with less common samples. The Oxford Brookes Centre for Bioimaging handles eukaryotic parasites, insect vectors and plant tissues. HyBiFa in Leeds has imaged colorectal cancer cells, kidney organoids and decellularised porcine tendon. In Newcastle upon Tyne, SpaRTAN applies spatial transcriptomics to tissue including human developmental material, while the Newcastle Biobank covers cancer, mitochondrial disease, neurodegenerative disease and COVID-19. The University of Leeds Atomic Force Microscopy Facility characterises polymers, pharmaceuticals and device materials alongside biological samples.

## Services and user support

A project with the Node usually begins with methodological set-up. Staff help design the study protocol and the standard operating procedures for the chosen method, so the experiment is matched to the question before imaging starts. Researchers can then be trained to use the infrastructure themselves, and training workstations support this learning. Those who would rather not run an instrument alone can work with staff, who provide technical assistance during acquisition.

Preparation work is supported as well. The Node offers probe preparation and wet lab space, and it has cell culture facilities at safety levels 1 and 2. Housing facilities are also part of what the Node provides, which helps projects that need samples maintained during the work. Once images are acquired, staff help with data processing and analysis, so results can be quantified with methods suited to the experiment. Server space is provided for the resulting data.

The Node is built from regional clusters, so a project can often combine several methods in one city. Oxford hosts four facilities. CICF and the Micron BioImaging Facility cover fluorescence and super-resolution light microscopy, while the Oxford Brookes Centre for Bioimaging adds electron tomography and serial block-face SEM. King's College London runs four facilities in London, pairing light microscopy at the Nikon Imaging Centre and the Wohl Cellular Imaging Centre with electron microscopy at KCEM. The CALM service at the Francis Crick Institute, also in London, contributes expertise in advanced light microscopy and image analysis.

Newcastle upon Tyne has the largest group, with seven facilities. The Newcastle University Bioimaging Unit provides light microscopy. SpaRTAN and the Newcastle University Genomics Core Facility link imaging to spatial transcriptomics and microdissection, and the Newcastle University Flow Cytometry Core Facility offers spatial proteomics. A bioinformatics unit, a biobank and a human developmental biology resource complete the cluster. In Liverpool, the University of Liverpool Centre for Cell Imaging is a containment-level-2 space that offers imaging at biosafety level above 1. The Biomedical Electron Microscopy Unit, also in Liverpool, adds serial-section TEM and Tokuyasu immunogold labelling. Further facilities in Leeds and Birmingham extend the light and electron microscopy offer. The OCTOPUS imaging cluster at Harwell combines laser-based imaging with cryo-correlative workflows, and UK-BIAS provides bioimage analysis as a distributed national service.
