# The UK Node

## Overview

The UK Node mainly offers advanced light microscopy, with super-resolution, live-cell and functional fluorescence imaging at its centre. Electron microscopy, spatial omics and image analysis complete the picture, and a national bioimage analysis service, UK-BIAS, works alongside the imaging facilities. The Node has 32 facilities at 15 institutions in ten cities between Edinburgh and London, and its biological imaging serves both cell biology and biomedical research.

The two ESRIC facilities in Edinburgh specialise in super-resolution microscopy. Their staff help researchers design single-molecule localisation, STED and structured illumination experiments in up to four colours, at resolutions down to 10 nm. The Microscopy Innovation Centre in London develops its own microscopy instruments and analysis tools. HyBiFa in Leeds images cells and tissues without labels, using coherent Raman scattering and harmonic generation. In electron microscopy, the Astbury Biostructure Laboratory Electron Microscopy Facility in Leeds runs cryo-EM structural workflows. Oxford Brookes Centre for Bioimaging in Oxford works with fixed or high-pressure-frozen plant tissues, parasites and insect vectors. The University of Birmingham Intravital Microscopy Suite in Birmingham images cells inside living animals.

Super-resolution light microscopy is centred on the two ESRIC facilities in Edinburgh, and single-molecule localisation is also offered in Oxford, Liverpool, York and London. Cryo-electron microscopy is at the Astbury Biostructure Laboratory Electron Microscopy Facility and the Biomedical Electron Microscopy Unit in Liverpool. Serial block-face electron microscopy is at KCEM in London, Oxford Brookes Centre for Bioimaging and the Biomedical Electron Microscopy Unit. FIB-SEM is at the Babraham Institute Imaging Facility near Cambridge and the OCTOPUS imaging cluster in Harwell. Spatial transcriptomics is at SpaRTAN in Newcastle upon Tyne and the University of York Imaging and Cytometry Laboratory in York.

## Imaging technologies offered

Confocal microscopy is offered by twelve facilities, and many of them add image scanning detectors that resolve finer detail than a standard confocal. Spinning disk confocals at six facilities give fast optical sectioning with less light on living cells. Light-sheet microscopy at six facilities images embryos, organs and other large specimens while sparing the planes not being recorded. The Oxford-ZEISS Centre of Excellence in Biomedical Imaging in Oxford adds a Zeiss Lattice Lightsheet 7 for 3D imaging of fast processes in single cells. Two-photon microscopy at five facilities reaches cells deep in thick tissue.

Functional methods show what molecules are doing. FLIM at eight facilities reads out the molecular environment and interactions, and FRET, FRAP and FCS measure proximity, mobility and diffusion. Automated high-content imaging at six facilities records cell phenotypes across many wells and conditions. Single-molecule localisation at eight facilities maps proteins below the diffraction limit, using instruments such as the Nikon N-STORM at the University of Edinburgh ESRIC site. STED is at the Heriot-Watt ESRIC site in Edinburgh, Micron BioImaging Facility and the Newcastle University Bioimaging Unit, with a Leica SP8 gSTED/TauSTED/FLIM in Edinburgh.

Electron microscopy covers cryo-TEM, serial block-face SEM and immunogold labelling on thawed cryo-sections at the Biomedical Electron Microscopy Unit. Array tomography and correlative light and electron microscopy are at the Babraham Institute Imaging Facility. Atomic force microscopy at the University of Leeds Atomic Force Microscopy Facility and the University of Liverpool Centre for Cell Imaging measures surface topography and cell mechanics, and the Bruker NanoWizard 4 in Leeds is coupled to confocal FLIM.

## Specialties and research focus

The research the facilities serve runs from single proteins to whole animals. The Astbury Biostructure Laboratory Electron Microscopy Facility works in structural biology on membrane proteins, ribosomes and viruses, with projects on Alzheimer's disease and DNA repair. COMPARE Advanced Imaging Facility in Birmingham is part of a centre for membrane proteins and receptors and supports work on receptor signalling. CICF in Oxford studies iPSC-derived neuronal organoids, mitochondrial movement, wound healing and macrophage motility.

The University of Birmingham Intravital Microscopy Suite works on cardiovascular disease, cancer, inflammation and thrombosis, and follows immune cells as they enter the liver, heart and kidney. In Newcastle upon Tyne, HDBR holds human embryonic and fetal tissue from three weeks of development, and SpaRTAN maps gene expression in tissue, including human developmental material. Newcastle Biobank stores donated samples for research on cancer, mitochondrial and neurodegenerative disease and COVID-19. HyBiFa works on colorectal cancer, kidney organoids, drug delivery and decellularised tendon. Oxford Brookes Centre for Bioimaging studies eukaryotic parasites, insect vectors and plant tissues. The Oxford-ZEISS Centre of Excellence in Biomedical Imaging serves inflammatory and musculoskeletal research, and the University of Leeds Atomic Force Microscopy Facility measures the mechanics of plant and mammalian cells.

## Services and user support

A project starts with methodological setup, where staff help design the study protocol and the standard operating procedures for the experiment. Researchers are then trained to use the instruments, and dedicated training workstations support this part of the work. Throughout the project, staff give technical assistance to run the instruments, so a visiting researcher can work alongside an expert rather than alone at the microscope.

The Node also prepares probes for labelling experiments. Visiting groups can use wet lab space and cell culture facilities at safety levels 1 and 2, which lets them handle and grow their samples on site, and the Node also provides housing facilities. Once images are acquired, staff help with data processing and analysis, and the Node provides server space for the resulting data.

Light microscopy facilities form the largest group. The two ESRIC facilities in Edinburgh focus on super-resolution microscopy and experimental design. Micron BioImaging Facility in Oxford combines advanced fluorescence microscopy with bioimage analysis, and CICF covers live-cell, high-content and super-resolution work. The Oxford-ZEISS Centre of Excellence in Biomedical Imaging is a partnership between the Kennedy Institute of Rheumatology and Carl Zeiss, and the Nikon Imaging Centre in London is run with Nikon Instruments UK. Confocal cores in Newcastle upon Tyne, Birmingham, Leeds, Liverpool and York complete the group. The University of Liverpool Centre for Cell Imaging is a containment level 2 space with temperature-controlled rooms for long-term live-cell imaging.

In electron microscopy, KCEM works at room temperature and under cryogenic conditions and adds cryo-SEM and elemental analysis. The Biomedical Electron Microscopy Unit covers 2D and 3D electron microscopy, correlative work and cryo-EM screening. The Babraham Institute Imaging Facility combines fluorescence microscopy with volume EM, and the OCTOPUS imaging cluster at the STFC Central Laser Facility offers laser-based imaging and FIB-SEM.

Analysis and spatial omics form a third group. UK-BIAS is a distributed national service for bioimage analysis, and the University of Liverpool Centre for Pre-Clinical Imaging offers image analysis for preclinical and ex vivo studies. In Newcastle upon Tyne, the Flow Cytometry Core Facility offers spatial proteomics and the Genomics Core Facility offers microdissection. CALM at the Francis Crick Institute in London brings expertise in advanced light microscopy and image analysis.
